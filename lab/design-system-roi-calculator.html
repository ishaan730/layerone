The method

The calculator counts only time saved, which is the part finance teams accept most easily. It leaves out quality, accessibility, brand consistency and the cost of change, so the result is a floor, not a ceiling.

Choosing honest assumptions

Published speed-ups are real but come from best-case tasks: Figma measured designers 34% faster on one task with a relevant system, and a study with IBM's Carbon found developers about 47% faster building one form. Across all of a team's UI work the gain is lower, which is why the default is 20%. Adoption is the other big lever: a system that only 40% of new work uses saves less than half as much as one at 90%.

The playbook example (40 engineers, 6 designers, a two-person system team) loses money in years one and two and turns a profit in year three, but on time savings alone it hasn't yet earned back the first two years by the end of year three. That's typical for a team of this size, and it's why design systems are best funded in stages, each earning the next, and why the other benefits belong in the business case too.

Questions

How do you calculate the ROI of a design system?

Estimate the hours spent on UI work, the share a system saves, and how much of the work uses the system each year. Multiply by the cost of an hour and compare with the cost of the team that runs the system.

How long does a design system take to pay back?

On time savings alone, a mid-sized product team often turns a yearly profit in year two or three and recovers the early losses later, because adoption ramps up slowly. Larger organisations pay back sooner.

What time saving should I assume?

Around 20% of UI work is a defensible default. Published figures like 34% or 47% come from single tasks and overstate the average.

Does it include quality and accessibility benefits?

No. It counts time saved only, so the result is a conservative floor. Mention the other benefits alongside it.
