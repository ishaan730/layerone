Work/Operations at CARS24

How the design system team stopped being the bottleneck

As CARS24 grew, every team needed something from the design system, and one small team couldn't keep up. We changed how work came in, got decided and got built, so teams moved fast without going around the system.

The setting

10+ products, three countries, one library

CARS24 runs buying, selling, loans and new cars across India, the UAE and Australia, on web, iOS and Android. Every one of those products used the same design system, looked after by one small team.

As the company grew, product teams needed changes faster than that team could make them. There was no clear way to ask for something, no fair way to decide what came first, and no way for teams to help. So everything waited.

Slower launches

Product work waited on library changes that had no date.

Paying twice

The same card, chip or carousel got built again by different teams.

Less trust

People buying a car worth lakhs saw screens that didn't match.

The problem

Six requests a week, one answered

Requests arrived through DMs, Slack threads, meetings and hallway chats. Nobody could see the full list, including the design system team. Whether the answer was yes depended on who asked and how loudly, so teams stopped trusting it and built their own versions.

No front door

Requests came in everywhere, so nobody could see them all.

No shared rules

Answers depended on who asked, so teams couldn't predict them.

No way to help

Teams that had already built something had nowhere to put it.

Research

First, we listened

Before designing any process, we looked at where things actually broke, from seven angles: interviews with designers, engineers and PMs; a workflow audit of where requests got stuck; usage data on unused and stalled requests; benchmarking against Base, Shopify Polaris and Agoda; surveys on trust in the process; shadowing to watch delays happen live; and experiments with existing frameworks.

The hardest part was deciding what to work on first. We scored a batch of real requests with RICE and MoSCoW. With RICE, people argued about the numbers, because reach and confidence are guesses for a shared component. With MoSCoW, every team's request came back as a Must.

For a shared component, it came down to four things: where it's used, who can reuse it, whether it already exists, and how hard it is to build.

Intake

Every request takes the same path

A bug, a new variant or a whole new component: it doesn't matter. Everything goes through the same seven steps, so anyone can tell where a request is and what happens next.

  1. Ask

    Post in the help channel or fill in the proposal form: what it's for, who needs it and how often it comes up.

    Anyone, any time

  2. Review

    New requests are reviewed together at weekly office hours. Anything unclear goes back as a question.

    DS team and requester, weekly

  3. Score

    Four questions, out of 70. The score is shared, so the requester sees why it landed where it did.

    DS team, in the open

  4. Decide

    Accept, defer or reject. Every decision goes back to the team with the reason attached.

    DS team, same week

  5. Build

    Core work is built by the DS team, extended work together, and custom work stays with the product team.

    Depends on the tier

  6. Release

    Documented in Figma and Storybook, announced with a changelog and migration steps.

    DS team, every release

  7. Check

    Live screens are audited every quarter. Anything that has drifted comes back in as a new request.

    DS team, quarterly

Step seven feeds step one, so the system keeps improving without anyone having to remember to ask.

Prioritisation

A score instead of an argument

Four plain questions that any product designer can answer without the design system team. Each answer is worth points, the points add up to a priority, and the priority decides what happens next. It works the same way for every team. Try it.

Where is it needed?

Work in main products helps the most people.

How many can reuse it?

A design system is for shared things.

Do we already have it?

Filling a real gap beats rebuilding what works.

How hard is it to build?

Quick wins go first, so teams aren't stuck waiting.

0 / 70
ScorePriorityWhat happens
55–70HighAccepted and picked up first. The design system team builds it.
40–54MediumAccepted, after high-priority work. The requesting team can contribute to speed it up.
20–39LowDeferred. The team can build it as a custom or extended component.
Any ✕Won't fixRejected, whatever the total. The team builds it outside the system using the design language.

It held up because the questions were ones requesters could answer themselves. Once requesters and the design system team kept landing on the same score, it became the default.

Contribution

Building with us, not waiting on us

Once people saw the system working, many of them wanted to add to it. Without a clear path that meant uneven quality, long reviews and confusion about ownership. So we wrote a four-step playbook, with a template for every step.

  1. Propose

    Start with context, not just a component: use cases, how often it comes up, the problem it solves, screenshots and suggested tokens.

    Requesting team

  2. Review and score

    Data, not debate. The proposal is scored with the same four questions as every other request.

    DS team, with the requester

  3. Build together

    Design system and product designers refine the spec together; engineers pair on reusable, tokenised code, checked for accessibility on web, iOS and Android.

    Both teams

  4. Document and release

    A component without documentation doesn't exist. It ships with docs, a version number and a release note.

    DS team

Around the playbook, we built listening into the routine at four speeds.

Every day

Slack channels for help, releases and changelogs. Fast answers, so nobody stays blocked.

Every week

Office hours where any designer brings real use cases, friction and ideas.

Every quarter

A system audit of live screens for token use, forked components and accessibility gaps.

Every release

Changelogs with migration steps, so platforms stop drifting apart.

Outcome

From gatekeeper to partner

  • Teams worked in silosA shared language and shared habits
  • Forked styles and duplicate workConsistency through tokens and the system
  • Rules handed down from the topContributions led by the community
  • The design system was a blockerThe design system was an enabler
  • Fear of changeTrust and collaboration

Designers trusted the tools and patterns. Engineers started suggesting improvements themselves. Product managers saw the system as a way to ship faster, and planned around releases. The design system team stopped being a bottleneck and became a facilitator.

Learnings

What we'd do again

Agree on the goal first

Every later argument is easier when everyone signed up to the same outcome.

Governance is what lets you scale

Without it, more demand just means a longer queue.

Docs and templates are lifelines

Templates made the right thing the easy thing.

Culture matters as much as code

The process only worked once people trusted it.
