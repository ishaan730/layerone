Work/Foundations at CARS24

One colour system for 10+ products in three countries

CARS24 grew from one product to more than six, across three countries, in about three years, and its design didn't keep up. We gave every team one colour palette and one set of design tokens, and that palette became the colour of the CARS24 rebrand.

Context

A company that grew faster than its design

CARS24 started as a place to buy and sell used cars. In three years it became a family of products, including loans, insurance, servicing and new cars, and it expanded from India into the UAE and Australia.

The teams grew just as fast. Design went from 20 people to 70, and engineering from about 100 to more than 200. Nothing shared held the work together, so each product and each country slowly built its own look.

The problem

Five codes for one colour

One shade of blue lived in the codebase as five different values. There was no central home for colour, type or spacing, so each team defined its own. Designers copied colours from old screens, developers copied them again in code, and the small differences kept piling up.

Design

Every team kept its own styles. Buttons, inputs, alerts and tabs looked different from product to product, and nothing was documented.

Engineering

With no shared UI code, each team rebuilt the same values. Handoffs broke and the same bugs kept coming back.

Brand

India, the UAE and Australia each had their own design language. New products launched with styles of their own.

People

Nobody owned the foundations, and with an average tenure of six to seven months, context kept walking out the door.

Research

Listening before changing anything

The existing setup was closer to a starter template than a system. Before touching it, we learned how teams were organised, where they got stuck, and where the business was heading over the next six to twelve months, including a rebrand.

We interviewed stakeholders, sat in on team rituals and looked at how styles were really used in files and in code. Then we brought design, engineering, marketing, brand, product managers and a few external users into the same workshops to map problems, desired outcomes and priorities.

The aim was a system people would want to use, not one they were told to use.

Colour

The colours had to go first

While planning the tokens, it became clear the colours themselves were the biggest problem. Many pairs failed basic contrast checks, nobody could say which colours were brand and which were UI, and the palette couldn't stretch across many products and markets.

A design system team doesn't change a brand colour on its own. We built a deck with the accessibility failures, the dated visuals and what designers had told us in interviews, took it to the Head of Design, and leadership agreed to a full overhaul.

We moved the CARS24 blue from the older, darker #0059A3 to an electric #4736FE that holds its contrast on screens and still prints true. Swatches were matched against Pantone cards, because printers, sign makers and vendors all work from Pantone.

To find the blue's partner we tested teal, pink and mint on the same real layouts, with the same photos and copy, so the only thing that changed was the colour. Mint won.

Colour science

Describing colour the way people see it

The old palette only existed as HEX and RGB, which tell a screen how much red, green and blue to mix but say nothing about how bright a colour looks to a person.

We specified the new palette in OKLCH as well. OKLCH describes colour by lightness, chroma and hue, and its lightness follows human perception. That made it much easier to build even ten-step ramps, predict contrast before testing, and keep colours steady between screen and print.

Every step of every ramp was checked against WCAG: AA as the minimum for product and marketing, AAA for important text and key actions.

Tokens

Four layers between a hex code and a button

We studied Shopify Polaris, Salesforce Lightning, IBM Carbon and Material. None fitted how CARS24 was organised: one company, three countries and several businesses sharing one system. So we built our own, and started with colour because the rebrand was about to change every colour anyway.

  1. What is it?

    Value

    #4736FE

    A raw colour. Nobody uses these directly.

  2. Which step of which ramp?

    Primitive

    color-blue-500

    Gives every value a name and a place in a scale.

  3. What is it for?

    Semantic

    color-surface-background-primary

    Names the purpose. Themes and dark mode swap values here.

  4. Exactly where is it used?

    Component

    button-background-primary

    Scoped to one component, so it can be tuned on its own.

The question we heard most in reviews was whether something should be a new semantic token. Too many and nobody can find the right one; too few and teams hard-code values. So we wrote two short rules: make it semantic when the idea is shared by several components, is about intent rather than colour, and is likely to change between themes. Keep it a component token when it's specific to one component and might need overriding.

Names were where teams disagreed most. One file had primaryBtnBg, another blue-button-new, and both meant the same thing. We agreed one grammar, with parts in a fixed order, and wrote documentation and onboarding so any team could extend it and contribute back.

Architecture

One system, many products

Everything sits on one base system. India is the default theme; each other country and business adds a theme that overrides only what it has to, with its name at the front of its tokens so an override is easy to spot.

Teams plug in at three levels, each a Figma library and a GitHub repository: the core system owned by the design system team, a pattern library per vertical, and shared pattern libraries for verticals that look alike. Components move up a level when more teams need them.

Core system

Tokens, components and docs. Owned by the design system team.

Vertical pattern libraries

Buy and sell, Loans, Australia, Dubai, New cars. Unique components for one business.

Shared pattern libraries

For verticals that look alike, such as Dubai and Australia.

Labs

Where foundation and pattern experiments are tested before they're promoted.

Impact

What one colour change costs

Say a colour used in more than a thousand places needs to change. At a mid-level designer's rate of $24 an hour, here's the same job done two ways.

By hand
~9 h, $216
With a token
30 s, $0.19

That's 99.9% less time on every colour change from then on. Across the company it added up to an estimated $156k a year in engineering time, and the rebrand rolled out 60% faster across three countries.

Learnings

What we'd tell ourselves at the start

Tie it to business goals

A system earns support when it clearly helps what the company is trying to do, and the success measures should show that.

Measure more than adoption

Time saved, cost avoided and quality gained tell a far better story than usage alone.

Show, don't tell

Demos and regular rituals changed more minds than any document.

Governance keeps it alive

Clear ownership and a clear token structure are what stop a system drifting again.
