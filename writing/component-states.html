Component states: designing every state before you build

A component looks perfect in the mock-up and falls apart in production, because real content and real people find the states nobody designed. The four groups of states, the combinations to decide, and the stress test that catches the rest.

The short answer

Every design system component needs more states than the mock-up shows. Design four groups for every component: interaction states (default, hover, pressed, focus, selected, disabled, read-only), data states (empty, loading, partly loaded, error, success), content extremes (too much, too little, other languages) and context (screen sizes, bigger text and zoom). Then decide which combinations are possible and which should be prevented. Test it all on a "stress test" page with real content, and don't hand the component to engineering until every state has a design.

  • A component is used in hundreds of places with content you'll never see. The states you forget are the ones that look broken in production.
  • Focus and disabled are the two states most often designed badly; focus must be clearly visible (WCAG 2.4.7).
  • Collect twenty real examples of the content before you design.

Why states matter more in a system

A screen designer designs for one situation. A system designer designs for every situation the component will ever meet, used by people they'll never meet, with content they'll never see. That's why a component that looks perfect in a mock-up can fall apart in a real product within a week.

An illustration: the alert that looked perfect until it met real messages

The situation

A designer creates a clean alert component. In the mock-up, every message is one short line: "Changes saved."

What goes wrong

In the product, a message reads "Your payment of ₹1,24,000 to Acme Supplies could not be processed because the bank declined the transaction. Try another payment method." The alert wraps onto four lines, the icon floats in the middle and the close button overlaps the text.

What would have helped

Collecting twenty real alert messages first, and testing the design with the longest, the shortest and the strangest of them.

The states, at a glance

Four groups of states to design

Interaction states

Most teams design default, hover and pressed and stop there. The two that matter most for quality are the ones skipped most often:

FOCUS

Visible, every time

Keyboard users need to see where they are. WCAG 2.4.7 requires a visible focus indicator, and WCAG 2.2 adds that it mustn't be hidden by other content. Use a focus ring with at least 3:1 contrast against the surrounding colours, and test it on every surface the component sits on, including dark ones.

DISABLED

Use it less than you think

Disabled controls often can't be focused, so screen reader users may not know they exist, and nobody is told why they can't use them. Where you can, keep the control enabled and explain what's missing when it's used. When you do disable, explain why nearby.

Read-only is different from disabled and deserves its own design: the value is real and can be selected and copied, it just can't be changed. Treating it as disabled makes important information look greyed-out and unimportant.

Data states

Any component that shows data needs a plan for when the data isn't normal. Decide them once in the system so every team doesn't improvise:

Content extremes

Real content is longer, shorter and stranger than mock-up content. Decide for each text area whether it wraps, truncates or scrolls, and write it down:

  • Wrap by default for anything people need to read in full: labels, messages, button text.
  • Truncate with "…" only where the full text is available another way (a tooltip, a detail page) and never for actions or errors.
  • Scroll inside the component for genuinely long content, like a long list in a menu, with a maximum height.
  • Plan for translation: many languages need noticeably more space than English. See localisation and RTL.

Combinations: allow, prevent or design

States combine. A field can be in error and disabled; a row can be selected and loading. For each component, list the combinations and decide which are possible, which should be prevented in code, and which need their own design.

The stress test page

Keep a page next to every component in your design file called "stress test". Put the component there with the longest real content, the shortest, an empty state, an error, a translated version and a phone-width version. Review that page in design critique, not the polished one. It takes about an hour per component and saves weeks of bug reports. Practitioner view

  1. Collect real content

    Twenty real examples from the product: the longest, shortest and oddest labels, messages and values.

  2. Design every state

    Work through the four groups and the combinations. Every colour and size comes from a token, including states.

  3. Build the stress test

    Long, short, empty, error, translated, phone-width and 200% zoom, side by side.

  4. Review with an engineer

    Ten minutes now ("can the menu open upwards when there's no space below?") prevents a week of rework.

  5. Hand over with the states listed

    The component isn't ready to build until every state has a design and a token.

States need tokens too

Every state's colours and sizes should come from tokens, so dark mode and themes change them correctly: action.primary.bg.hover, border.focus, text.disabled. Put the state last in the name. See token naming conventions and component tokens.

In practice

At CARS24, the mobile component system tested look and feel on real screens before anything was built, so states were checked against real listings and prices in every market. Read the case study.

The short version

  • Design four groups: interaction, data, content extremes and context.
  • Focus must always be visible; use disabled sparingly and explain it.
  • Decide which combinations to allow, prevent or design.
  • Review a stress test page with real content, not the polished mock-up.
  • Every state gets a token, with the state last in the name.

Frequently asked questions

What states should a design system component have?

Interaction states (default, hover, pressed, focus, selected, disabled, read-only), data states (empty, loading, error, success), content extremes (long, short, translated) and context (screen sizes and zoom), plus any combinations that can occur.

What's the difference between disabled and read-only?

Read-only shows a real value people can select and copy but not change. Disabled means the control can't be used at all right now. They need different designs.

Should disabled buttons be used?

Sparingly. They often can't be focused and don't explain why they're unavailable. Prefer an enabled button that explains what's missing when pressed.

How do I test component states?

A stress test page in the design file, and a story for every state in the component workshop, with visual tests that compare them on every change.
