The short answer

Design system governance is the agreed way decisions about the system get made: who decides what, how, how fast, and how it's recorded. Good governance is light. It lets most decisions happen quickly, by the person closest to the work, and slows down only for the few that affect everyone. Its core parts are a decision-rights matrix, processes matched to the size of each decision, an escalation path, automated quality gates and a decision log.

  • One decider per kind of decision. "The team decides" means nobody does.
  • Give accessibility a real veto: someone who can block a change that fails the standard.
  • Grow governance with the organisation, never ahead of it.

What design system governance is, and isn't

Governance has a bad reputation, because people picture committees and approval forms. Good design system governance is the opposite: it's what lets a token change, a new variant or a deprecation happen in days rather than drift for months.

An illustration: three months to change a border radius

The situation

Brand wants rounder corners. The system designer agrees. An engineering lead worries about layouts. A product director wants to wait until after a launch.

What goes wrong

Nobody knows who decides. The topic comes up in five meetings over three months. Everyone's frustrated; nothing happens.

What governance changes

Token changes are decided by the system lead after a one-week comment period. The engineering concern becomes a release plan; the launch sets the date. Decided in ten days and logged with reasons.

Brad Frost's widely used governance process makes the same point a different way: most work should flow through a clear, predictable path from "use what's there" to "propose a change" to "release", with the system team and product teams each knowing their part. Governance is that path, plus clarity about who decides at each step.

Why governance fails

TOO LITTLE

Nobody decides

Decisions are re-opened in every meeting. The loudest team gets its way. Components fork because changing the original is too uncertain. Quality drifts because nobody can say no.

TOO MUCH

Everything waits

Every change goes to a monthly council, so everything takes a month. Teams stop asking and build locally. The system becomes something to work around.

The aim is the narrow path between them: fast for small, reversible decisions; careful for big, hard-to-undo ones.

Notice the accessibility veto. It's deliberate: accessibility is the one area where "we'll fix it later" multiplies harm across every product that uses the system.

Matching the process to the decision

Not every decision needs the same process. Ask two questions: how many people does this affect, and how easy is it to undo?

The comment period matters as much as the decision. People accept decisions they disagree with far more readily when they had a genuine chance to be heard and the reasons are written down.

When people disagree: the escalation path

Disagreement is healthy; endless disagreement isn't. Publish an escalation path, with time limits, so nobody has to invent one in the middle of an argument.

The decision log

Two simple records make governance trustworthy: a decision log that records what was decided and why, and an ownership registry that lists who owns each component, pattern and token group. The log stops decisions being re-litigated and gives newcomers the history behind the system.

A mistake we see often

A council for everything. Councils meet monthly, so everything takes a month. Keep the council for the handful of decisions that genuinely cross business units, and let the decision-rights matrix handle the rest.

The short version

  • Governance makes most decisions fast and slows down only the few that affect everyone.
  • Write down one decider for each kind of decision, and give accessibility a real veto.
  • Match the process to the impact, publish an escalation path, and expect everyone to disagree and commit.
  • Automate standards as gates; keep a decision log and an ownership registry.
  • Grow governance only as you grow.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between centralised and federated governance?

In a centralised model a dedicated team makes and builds the decisions. In a federated model, contributors across product teams do, with no full-time owner. Most mature systems end up hybrid: a core team owns quality and direction, and product teams contribute.

Who has the final say on a design system?

It depends on the decision. The system lead usually decides foundations, breaking changes and deprecations; the engineering lead decides architecture and token structure; leadership decides roadmap and funding. Write it down in a decision-rights matrix.

How do you stop governance slowing teams down?

Match the process to the size of the decision, automate standards as gates, and keep councils for genuinely cross-company decisions. Everyday changes should be decided by the owner in review, in days.

How should we handle teams that detach or fork components?

Treat it as a signal, not a crime. Track detaches, ask why, and either improve the component or offer an exception with an expiry date. Persistent forking usually means requests are too slow.

Do small teams need governance?

A little: two named owners, decisions written in the channel, and code review as the gate. That's governance too, and it's enough until there are more teams than the owners can talk to.
